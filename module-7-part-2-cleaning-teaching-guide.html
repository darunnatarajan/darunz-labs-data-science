7.7 Missing value treatment

Real datasets have holes in them. scikit-learn refuses to train on a single NaN, so every project starts here. The choice is never "fill it and move on": it's decide why it's missing, then choose the least damaging fix.

What is NA?

A cell where no value was recorded. Not zero, not blank text: nothing at all.

pandas shows missing numbers as NaN and missing objects as None or NaT for dates. Whatever the label, the meaning is the same: the value was never captured.

Before fixing anything, ask why it's missing. The answer decides the fix:

Why it's missingExample in this dataSafe to fill?
Random slipThe field team forgot to type F14's rentYes, filling is fine
Depends on something elseRents missing only for flats visited on a rainy dayFill, but use the related columns
Missing for a reasonOwners of very expensive flats refuse to share rentCareful. Filling hides the pattern; add a flag column

Say it in class

"Attendance register-la oru kattam kaaliya irukku. Student varala-nu artham-aa, illa sir kaiyezhuthu podave marandhutaara-nu artham-aa? Rendum vera vera. NA paathaa, 'edhukku miss aachu' nu dhaan mudhalla kekkanum. Adha kekkaama fill panna koodaadhu."

Real-time example

Measuring the damage first

Rent: 3 missing out of 15 = 20%
Sq ft: 1 missing out of 15 = 6.7%
Rows with no missing value at all: 11 of 15

Rough guide for what the percentage means:

MissingUsual move

5% to 30%Fill carefully, using the other columns
Over 50%Consider dropping the whole column, or keep it only as a yes/no flag

Rent at 20% is the interesting case: too much to throw away, too much to fill carelessly.

Python

t.isna().sum()             # sqft 1, rent 3
t.isna().mean() * 100      # rent 20.0 percent
t.dropna().shape           # (11, 5)  rows with nothing missing
t[t["rent"].isna()]        # the three rows to investigate: F05, F09, F14

# Missing values disguised as text when the CSV was read:
pd.read_csv("flats.csv", na_values=["NA", "na", "-", "?", "", "null"])

Where it's used

The first three lines of every EDA notebook, and the first question in most data science interviews.

Watch out

Zero is not missing. A flat with rent 0 is a data error or a free stay; a flat with NaN was simply never recorded. If someone fills missing values with 0, every average in the project is wrong from that point on.

Drop or fill?

Dropping throws away whole rows to get rid of a few holes. Filling keeps the rows but invents the missing numbers.

Neither is free. Dropping costs you data, which hurts most when the dataset is small. Filling costs you honesty: the filled values look like real measurements but are guesses.

Say it in class

"Oru row-la onnu rendu cell miss aayiruntha, full row-aiyum thookki pottaa, andha row-la irukkura மீதி nalla data ellaam waste. Aduthadhu yosikkaama delete pannadheenga."

The rule I teach

Few missing rows, plenty of data → drop the rows
Column mostly empty → drop the column
Everything else → fill, and note what you filledNever drop or fill before you have looked at why the values are missing.

Real-time example

What dropping costs on 15 flats

Start: 15 flats
After dropna(): 11 flats
Lost: 4 flats, 27% of the data

Losing 27% of a small dataset is expensive, and the flats you lose are not random: F05 and F15 are both small Tambaram flats, so the survivors lean towards bigger, costlier homes. On a dataset of 50,000 rows, dropping 4 would be the easy call. Size decides.

Python

t.dropna()                            # drops any row with any missing value: 11 left
t.dropna(subset=["rent"])             # only rows whose rent is missing: 12 left
t.dropna(axis=1)                      # drops the whole column instead
t.dropna(thresh=4)                    # keep rows with at least 4 real values

t["rent_was_missing"] = t["rent"].isna().astype(int)   # keep the fact, then fill

Watch out

dropna() returns a new DataFrame; it does not change the original unless you assign it back. Half the "my code did nothing" questions in class are this line.

Central imputation

Fill the holes with the column's middle value: mean or median for numbers, mode for categories.

It's the fastest fix and the one every learner reaches for first. The skill is knowing which centre to use, and that comes straight from part 1: mean for roughly symmetric columns, median when the column is skewed or has outliers, mode for text columns. Rent here is heavily right-skewed with a ₹95,000 flat sitting in it, so the median is the honest choice.

Say it in class

"Oru student mark miss aayiduchu-nu vachukonga. Class average-ai pottutu போகலாம். Romba easy. Aana andha student-oda real mark adhu illa, and ellaa miss aana students-ukkum ore mark poduvom. Adhanaala class-oda spread suruki povum."

Formula

Numeric, symmetric: fill with the mean
Numeric, skewed or with outliers: fill with the median
Categorical: fill with the mode, or with the label "Unknown"

Real-time example

Mean or median for the three missing rents?

The 12 rents we do have:

Mean = ₹28,333   Median = ₹22,500   Mode = ₹18,000   SD = ₹23,031

The mean sits above the median, which tells you the column is right-skewed (part 1, unit 7.1). The ₹95,000 Adyar flat is doing that. Filling three small Tambaram and OMR flats with ₹28,333 would give them rents higher than most real flats in the table.

After fillingMeanMedianSD

Filled with the mean28,33328,00020,415

Look at the SD column. Both fills pull it down by around ₹2,500, because three identical values were added at the centre. That is the cost of central imputation: the data looks more consistent than it really is.

Python

t["rent"] = t["rent"].fillna(t["rent"].median())      # 22500 in all three holes
t["locality"] = t["locality"].fillna(t["locality"].mode()[0])

# The scikit-learn way, which remembers the value for the test set:
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
imputer = SimpleImputer(strategy="median")            # mean | median | most_frequent | constant
X_train[["rent"]] = imputer.fit_transform(X_train[["rent"]])
X_test[["rent"]]  = imputer.transform(X_test[["rent"]])   # transform only, never fit again

Where it's used

The default first pass in almost every project, and inside Pipeline objects in production code.

Watch out

Fit the imputer on the training set only. If you calculate the median over the full dataset and then split, test-set information has leaked into training, and your accuracy score will be better in the notebook than in real life.

KNN imputation

Instead of the whole column's average, use the average of the few rows that look most like this one.

This is unit 7.5 doing real work. Measure the distance from the incomplete row to every complete row using the other columns, take the k nearest neighbours, and average their values for the missing field.

Say it in class

"Andha student-oda mark theriyala. Class average podadheenga. Avanoda close friends yaaru, same group, same level-la padikkuravanga yaaru-nu paarunga. Avanga marks-oda average dhaan romba nalla guess. Adhu dhaan KNN imputation."

Formula

1. Scale every numeric column first
2. Distance from the incomplete row to each complete row (Euclidean, unit 7.5)
3. Take the k nearest rows, usually k = 3 or 5
4. Fill with the average of their valuesStep 1 is not optional. Without scaling, sq ft decides every neighbour and BHK is ignored.

Real-time example

Python

from sklearn.impute import KNNImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

num = t[["bhk", "sqft", "rent"]]
scaler = StandardScaler()
Z = scaler.fit_transform(num)                       # scale first
filled = scaler.inverse_transform(
    KNNImputer(n_neighbors=3).fit_transform(Z))     # impute, then scale back

# F05 10,667   F09 16,000   F14 21,000   F12 sqft 967

Where it's used

Datasets where rows differ a lot from each other: house prices, patient records, customer profiles. Anywhere a single column average would be too blunt.

Watch out

KNN imputation is slow on large datasets because it measures distance to every other row, and it needs the other columns to be informative. If the remaining columns are unrelated to the missing one, the neighbours are meaningless and the median is just as good with none of the cost.

Checkpoint 7.7

Ask these before starting 7.8.

Q1. A column has 3 missing values out of 5,000 rows. What's the simplest reasonable fix?

Drop those three rows. At 0.06% of the data, nothing is lost and no values are invented.

Q2. Salary is missing for 15% of employees and the column is right-skewed. Mean or median?

Median. The mean is dragged up by a few very high salaries, so it would overstate every filled row.

Q3. Why must you scale before KNN imputation?

Distance is dominated by whichever column has the biggest numbers. Unscaled, sq ft (hundreds to thousands) decides every neighbour and BHK (1 to 4) is ignored. Same trap as unit 7.5.

Q4. A learner fills missing values using the median of the whole dataset, then does the train/test split. What's wrong?

Data leakage. The median carries information from the test rows into training, so the test score is optimistic. Split first, fit the imputer on train, then transform test.

Q5. Customers who did not give their income earn much more on average than those who did. What should you do?

Do not just fill it. The missingness itself carries information, so add a flag column like income_missing and then fill. The model can use the flag.

7.8 Encoding and scaling

A model only reads numbers, and it reads them literally. Text columns have to become numbers without inventing an order, and numeric columns often have to be put on a common ruler.

About 75 minutes

Dummification (one-hot encoding)

Turn one text column into several yes/no columns, one for each category.

The obvious shortcut is to number the categories: Adyar 1, Anna Nagar 2, OMR 3, Tambaram 4, Velachery 5. The model then believes Velachery is five times Adyar and that OMR sits exactly between Anna Nagar and Tambaram. None of that is true. Localities have no order, so they must not be given one.

Dummification avoids it: each locality gets its own column holding 1 or 0.

Say it in class

"Model-kku 'Velachery' nu sonnaa puriyaadhu, number dhaan puriyum. Aana Velachery = 1, Adyar = 2 nu pottaa, Adyar-ku rendu madangu mathippu-nu model nenachudum. Adhukku badhila 'Velachery-aa? aamaa illa illa-yaa?' nu ovvoru area-kkum thani column kudunga."

Which encoding when

Type of columnEncodingExample
No order (nominal)One-hot / dummiesLocality, gender, payment method
Real order (ordinal)Label or ordinal encodingLow < Medium < High, ratings 1 to 5
The target columnLabel encodingChurn: Yes → 1, No → 0
Very many categoriesGroup the rare ones, or use frequency encodingPin codes, product IDs

Real-time example

Encoding the locality column

Five localities appear in the data: Adyar, Anna Nagar, OMR, Tambaram and Velachery. One-hot encoding turns one column into five.

Drop one column. With drop_first=True the Adyar column disappears and a flat with 0 in all four remaining columns is an Adyar flat. Four columns carry the same information as five. Keeping all five makes them perfectly predictable from each other, which upsets linear and logistic regression; this is the dummy variable trap. Tree models don't care either way.

5 localities → 5 dummy columns → 4 after dropping the first

Python

pd.get_dummies(flats, columns=["locality"], dtype=int)
pd.get_dummies(flats, columns=["locality"], drop_first=True, dtype=int)

# Ordinal, where the order is real:
flats["size_band"] = flats["size_band"].map({"Small": 0, "Medium": 1, "Large": 2})

# The scikit-learn way, which remembers the categories for new data:
from sklearn.preprocessing import OneHotEncoder
enc = OneHotEncoder(drop="first", handle_unknown="ignore", sparse_output=False)
enc.fit_transform(X_train[["locality"]])

Where it's used

Before every linear or logistic regression, inside ColumnTransformer pipelines, and in feature engineering for almost any tabular model.

Watch out

A column with thousands of categories, like pin code, produces thousands of columns and a model that memorises noise. Group rare categories into "Other" first. And if a locality appears in the test set that was never in training, OneHotEncoder with handle_unknown="ignore" handles it; pd.get_dummies silently gives you mismatched columns.

Feature scaling

Put every numeric column on the same ruler, so a big-numbered column can't shout down a small-numbered one.

Sq ft runs from 550 to 3,200. BHK runs from 1 to 4. Any method that measures distance, or that adds up weighted features, will hear sq ft and almost nothing else. Unit 7.5 showed exactly this with age and income.

Say it in class

"Oruthar height-ai centimeter-la, innoruthar weight-ai kilo-la sollitu, rendaiyum koota mudiyaadhu. Mudhalla rendaiyum ore alavukolla kondu vaanga. Adhu dhaan scaling."

Formula

Standardisation (StandardScaler): z = (x − mean) / SDCentres on 0, typical range about −3 to +3. Outliers survive, which is usually what you want.
Min-max (MinMaxScaler): x′ = (x − min) / (max − min)Squeezes everything into 0 to 1. One outlier pushes every other value into a tiny corner.

Real-time example

Scaling three flats

Sq ft: mean 1,166.7, SD 614.2  |  BHK: mean 2.2, SD 0.83

Two things to point at on the screen. First, F11's sq ft z-score of 3.31 says it is more than three SDs above average, exactly the outlier from unit 7.6. Second, under min-max that outlier is pinned at 1.00 and everything else is squashed below 0.34, which is why min-max and outliers are a bad pairing.

ModelScaling needed?Why

Python

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler

scaler = StandardScaler()
X_train_s = scaler.fit_transform(X_train)   # learn mean and SD here
X_test_s  = scaler.transform(X_test)        # reuse them here

scaler.mean_       # bhk 2.2, sqft 1166.7
scaler.scale_      # bhk 0.833, sqft 614.2  (population SD, ddof=0)

Where it's used

Before KNN, K-Means, SVM and PCA, inside KNN imputation as you saw in 7.7, and as a standard step in every scikit-learn Pipeline.

Watch out

Two traps, both common in interviews:

Scale after splitting. fit_transform on training, transform on test. Fitting the scaler on the whole dataset leaks the test set's mean and SD into training.

Don't scale the target. Scale the features, leave y alone, or your predictions come out in z-scores instead of rupees.

Also note scaler.scale_ for sq ft is 614.2, while flats["sqft"].std() gives 635.7. scikit-learn divides by N and pandas by n − 1, the same gotcha as unit 7.3.

Checkpoint 7.8

Ask these before starting 7.9.

Q1. A column holds Red, Green and Blue. A learner maps them to 1, 2, 3. What breaks?

The model now thinks Blue is three times Red and that Green sits between them. Colours have no order, so use one-hot encoding.

Q2. Education level is School, Graduate, Post-graduate. One-hot or label encoding?

Label or ordinal encoding (0, 1, 2). Here the order is real and worth keeping.

Q3. Why does drop_first=True exist?

With all the dummy columns present, any one of them can be worked out from the others, which destabilises regression coefficients. Dropping one makes that category the baseline. Trees are unaffected.

Q4. Do you need to scale features for a Random Forest?

No. Trees split one column at a time, so the scale of each column is irrelevant. KNN, K-Means, SVM and regularised regression do need it.

Q5. Your data has one huge outlier. MinMaxScaler or StandardScaler?

StandardScaler, or RobustScaler which uses the median and IQR. Min-max pins the outlier at 1 and crushes everything else into a small band.

7.9 Scatter plot and correlation

Everything so far described one column at a time. This unit is about pairs: when one column goes up, what does the other one do? That question is the whole basis of regression, and of choosing which features to feed a model.

About 75 minutes

Scatter plot

One dot per row, placed by two columns at once. The shape of the cloud is the answer.

Read every scatter plot in four steps, and make the class say them out loud:

  1. Direction: does the cloud rise, fall, or neither?
  2. Shape: a straight band, a curve, or no pattern?
  3. Strength: a tight line or a loose cloud?
  4. Odd ones out: any dot sitting far from the rest?

Say it in class

"Ovvoru pulli-yum oru flat. Idathula size, mela vadagai. Pulli ellaam mela yeri poitu irundhaa, 'periya veedu, adhika vadagai'. Padam-ey answer-ai sollidum, calculation kooda thevai illa."

Real-time example

Size against rent, for our 15 flats

Plot sq ft on the x-axis and rent on the y-axis, as in the picture at the top of this guide. The dots climb steadily from left to right, so bigger flats cost more. One dot sits far out on its own: the 3,200 sq ft Adyar flat at ₹95,000.

Now cover that one dot with your thumb and ask the class whether the pattern still holds. It does. That's the difference between an outlier that creates a pattern and one that merely sits at the end of a real one.

Python

import seaborn as sns
sns.scatterplot(data=flats, x="sqft", y="rent")
sns.scatterplot(data=flats, x="sqft", y="rent", hue="locality")   # colour by a third column
sns.pairplot(flats[["bhk", "sqft", "rent"]])                      # every pair at once

Where it's used

The standard first look at any two numeric columns, checking regression assumptions, and spotting clusters before running K-Means.

Watch out

With thousands of rows the dots pile on top of each other and the plot turns into a solid blob. Use alpha=0.3, or a hexbin or 2-D density plot instead.

Pearson correlation

One number from −1 to +1 that says how close the dots sit to a straight line, and whether that line rises or falls.

The sign is the direction and the size is the strength. It is the scatter plot squeezed into a single number, which is what makes it useful across dozens of columns at once, and also what makes it easy to misuse.

Say it in class

"r = +1 nu sonnaa, rendum perfect-aa ore direction-la. r = −1 nu sonnaa, onnu yeruchu na innonnu erangum. r = 0 nu sonnaa, relationship-ey illa. Padathula pakkiradhu dhaan, aana oru number-la."

Formula

r = Σ(x − x)(y − ȳ) / √[ Σ(x − x)² × Σ(y − ȳ)² ]Each column's deviations from its own mean (unit 7.3), multiplied together and standardised. No units, so r never changes if you switch sq ft to square metres.

|r|StrengthWhat the scatter looks like
0.0 – 0.2Very weak or noneA shapeless cloud
0.2 – 0.4WeakA hint of a slope
0.4 – 0.6ModerateA visible but loose band
0.6 – 0.8StrongA clear band

Real-time example

The correlation matrix for our flats

BHKSq ftRent
BHK1.000.880.84

Reading it: sq ft and rent at 0.99 is very strong, so sq ft alone would predict rent well. BHK and rent at 0.84 is also strong. But BHK and sq ft at 0.88 means the two features largely repeat each other, which is called multicollinearity; in a linear regression you would keep one or combine them.

Now drop the ₹95,000 flat and recalculate:

Sq ft and rent: 0.99 → 0.94
BHK and rent: 0.84 → 0.91
BHK and sq ft: 0.88 → 0.97

One row out of fifteen moved every number, and not all in the same direction. Correlation is as sensitive to outliers as the mean is.

Two quick ones for the board

Hours studied against marks for ten students: r ≈ 0.98, a very strong positive relationship. Price against units sold for a product: r ≈ −0.96, a very strong negative one. Same strength, opposite directions.

r squared: square the correlation to get the share of variation explained. For sq ft and rent without the outlier, 0.94² = 0.88, so about 88% of the variation in rent goes with the variation in size. You will meet this again as the R² score of a regression model.

Try it live

Python

flats["sqft"].corr(flats["rent"])        # 0.99

Where it's used

Feature selection, spotting multicollinearity before linear regression, and quick sanity checks: if a feature has near-zero correlation with the target, ask whether it belongs in the model at all.

Watch out

A correlation near zero does not mean "no relationship". It means no straight-line relationship. The next topic shows a perfect curved relationship with r = 0.00.

Correlation is not causation

Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other. Something else may be moving both.

Say it in class

"Ice cream sales yeruchu, adhe time-la drowning cases-um yeruchu. Ice cream saapttaa moozhgiduvaangala? Illa. Rendaiyum yetharadhu veyil kaalam. Adhu dhaan lurking variable."

Real-time example

Three explanations for the same correlation

Suppose AC sales and cold drink sales in Chennai correlate at 0.9. There are three possibilities, and the number alone cannot tell you which:

ExplanationWhat it means here
X causes YBuying an AC makes people thirsty. Unlikely
Y causes XDrinking cold drinks makes people buy ACs. Unlikely
Z causes bothSummer heat drives both. This is the real answer

A fourth possibility exists in small datasets: coincidence. With enough columns, some pair will correlate strongly by pure chance.

A business version: customers who use the mobile app spend more, so the marketing team wants everyone pushed onto the app. But heavy spenders may simply be the kind of customers who install apps. Only an experiment, where you randomly encourage half of them, can answer it. That is exactly why A/B tests exist.

The three questions to ask

1. Could a third factor be driving both?
2. Could the direction be the other way round?
3. Could this be a curve, or the work of a few outliers?If you cannot rule all three out, say "associated with", not "causes".

Where it's used

Every business recommendation a data scientist writes. It is also the most commonly asked reasoning question in analyst interviews.

Watch out

Pearson also assumes numbers. Running .corr() on encoded categories (Adyar = 1, Velachery = 5) produces a number that looks meaningful and is not. For two categorical columns use a crosstab and a chi-square test instead.

Checkpoint 7.9

Ask these before the practice assignment.

Q1. What does r = −0.85 tell you?

A strong negative relationship: as one column rises, the other falls, with the dots close to a downward straight line.

Q2. Two columns have r = 0.02, but the scatter is a clear U shape. Explain.

The relationship is real but curved, and Pearson only measures straight lines. Plot the data, or use Spearman, or fit a model that handles curves.

Q3. Sq ft and BHK correlate at 0.88. Why might you not put both into a linear regression?

Multicollinearity. They carry much the same information, which makes the coefficients unstable and hard to interpret. Keep one, or combine them.

Q4. "Students who attend more classes score higher, so attendance causes marks." What's the flaw?

A third factor, motivation, likely drives both attendance and marks. The two are associated; that does not prove one causes the other.

Q5. Sq ft and rent correlate at 0.94. What share of the variation in rent goes with size?

0.94² = 0.88, so about 88%.

Tasks

1. How many values are missing in each column, and what percentage? (7.7)

Fare: 3 of 14 = 21.4%. Distance: 1 of 14 = 7.1%. Everything else is complete.

2. How many rows survive dropna()? Is dropping a good idea here? (7.7)

10 of 14 rows survive, so dropping loses 29% of a small dataset. Worse, three of the four lost rides are the longest and the shortest trips, so the survivors are all mid-range. Fill instead.

3. Find the mean, median and SD of the fares you do have. Should you fill with the mean or the median? (7.1, 7.7)

Of the 11 known fares: mean ₹171.8, median ₹160, SD ₹72.9. The mean sits above the median, so the column leans right. The median is the safer single-value fill here, though neither is ideal, which task 5 shows.

4. Fill the fares with the mean, then with the median. What happens to the spread? (7.3, 7.7)

Mean fill: mean ₹171.8, median ₹171.8, SD ₹63.9. Median fill: mean ₹169.3, median ₹160, SD ₹64.1.

The SD drops from ₹72.9 to about ₹64 either way, because three identical values were added at the centre. Central imputation always makes data look tidier than it is.

5. Use KNN imputation with k = 3 on distance, duration and fare. What does R05 get, and why is it better than the median? (7.5, 7.7)

R05 is a 15 km, 46 minute ride: the longest in the table. Its three nearest rides are R12 (₹285), R02 (₹275) and R08 (₹255).

(285 + 275 + 255) ÷ 3 = ₹271.7

The median would have given it ₹160, less than a 6 km ride. KNN also fills R11 at ₹200, R13 at ₹90, and R09's missing distance at 3.2 km.

6. Encode the area column. How many columns do you end up with, and which one is the baseline? (7.8)

Four areas, so four dummy columns, or three with drop_first=True. The dropped one (Adyar, alphabetically first) becomes the baseline: a ride with 0 in all three remaining columns is an Adyar ride. Area has no order, so label encoding would be wrong.

7. Standardise distance and duration. What are the z-scores for R01 and R05? (7.3, 7.8)

Distance: mean 7.88, SD 3.77. Duration: mean 25.07, SD 11.27.

R01 (4 km, 14 min): z = −1.03 and −0.98. R05 (15 km, 46 min): z = 1.89 and 1.86. Both rides sit about the same distance from average on both features, which is exactly what scaling is for.

8. Build the correlation matrix. What would you feed a fare-prediction model? (7.9)

Distance and fare 0.99, duration and fare 0.99, distance and duration 0.99. Everything moves together, because cab fares are calculated from distance in the first place.

For a linear model, keep one of distance or duration rather than both: at 0.99 they are near-duplicates and the coefficients would be unstable. Distance is the natural choice.

9. A colleague says "longer rides cause higher fares, so tell drivers to take longer routes." What's wrong? (7.9)

Distance does drive the fare here, since the meter uses it. But the recommendation confuses a billing rule with a business lever: longer routes mean unhappy customers, not more profit. The lesson is that a strong correlation tells you what moves together, never what you should do about it.
